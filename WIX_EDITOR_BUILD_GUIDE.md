# 2. Site-wide visual texture and design system

## Overall texture

Use a layered, immersive look that echoes Project 1. The design should suggest depth, perspective, and the idea of looking beyond a surface-level frame.

Avoid:

- Puzzle-piece imagery
- Bright clinical white backgrounds
- Overly medical or diagnostic visuals
- Crowded text blocks

Use:

- Deep background colors
- Warm accent buttons
- Soft abstract gradients
- Large image areas
- Rounded cards
- Generous spacing

## Suggested color palette

Use these colors or close Wix equivalents:

- Background plum/black: `#170D18`
- Deep purple: `#3A173D`
- Aubergine: `#56204F`
- Warm rose: `#B55B73`
- Gold accent: `#E0A458`
- Soft teal accent: `#70B7AE`
- Cream text: `#F8EFE6`
- Muted body text: `#D9C8BC`

## Typography

Use one clean sans-serif family across the site.

Suggested Wix font pairings:

- Headings: **Avenir Next**, **Montserrat**, or **Poppins**
- Body: **Open Sans**, **Lato**, or **Avenir**

Heading style:

- Large
- Bold
- Slightly dramatic
- Plenty of line spacing

Body style:

- Medium size
- Easy to read
- High contrast against dark background

## Background texture in Wix

For each page:

1. Set the page background to deep plum/black.
2. Add one or two large abstract shapes or gradient image strips.
3. Lower opacity to about 20-35%.
4. Place them behind text sections.
5. Use rounded containers with semi-transparent dark backgrounds for cards.

Simple Wix texture formula:

- Page background: deep plum
- Hero strip: dark gradient or Project 1 cropped image
- Cards: semi-transparent dark purple
- Buttons: gold fill with dark text
- Secondary buttons: transparent fill, cream border

## Accessibility notes

- Add alt text to every image.
- Keep text contrast high.
- Do not place long paragraphs over busy images.
- Use clear button labels such as "View Project" and "Explore the Projects."
- Check the mobile layout before publishing.

**Medium line:**

## Image section

Place the full Project 1 image near the top of the page, before the longer writing.

**Image alt text:**

Layered photo composite contrasting a phone caption reading "This is Autism" with fuller overlapping images of a person in the background.

## Artist's statement

**Heading:**

Artist's Statement

**Copy:**

This project started with a question: what does it mean to say "this is autism"? I wanted to challenge the way autism gets flattened in media into a single, recognizable image: a character type, a behavior, a diagnosis, as if one frame could capture the full complexity of a person. My audience was young adults who consume a lot of social media and may have absorbed these portrayals without ever stopping to question them.

To explore this, I created a layered composite image that contrasts two versions of the same person. In the foreground, someone holds a phone and frames a girl for a social media post. The screen reads "This is Autism," reducing her identity to a single statement. In the background, that same girl appears in multiple overlapping moments: posing, performing, laughing, existing in the fullness of her own life. The glowing, high-contrast edits on her central portrait suggest that even when we think we are seeing someone clearly, our view is still filtered through assumptions. The swirling, immersive background is everything the phone screen cannot hold.

## Rhetorical reflection

**Heading:**

Rhetorical Reflection

**Copy:**

The central rhetorical move in this project is juxtaposition. The flat, controlled foreground, including the phone, caption, and sense of certainty, sits in direct tension with the layered, expressive background. Editing choices reinforced this contrast: the background feels alive and immersive through layer blending, filters, and color, while the foreground feels cropped and restricted.

The text "This is Autism" sounds confident and final, but the image around it immediately complicates that confidence. Rather than arguing against stereotypes directly, the composition invites viewers to feel the inadequacy of the label themselves.

**Viewer instruction:**

Click through the graphic to explore each misconception.

**Screenshot alt text if using a preview image:**

Screenshot preview of the Misconceptions Uncovered interactive Telar project.

## Artist's statement

**Heading:**

Artist's Statement

**Copy:**

For this project, I wanted to go deeper than a single image. I wanted viewers to actually move through the argument. Using Telar's zoom-based format, I created an interactive graphic that begins with broad, familiar media portrayals of autism and gradually zooms in on specific stereotypes, examples, and lived experiences. The structure mirrors the project's core message: understanding autism means looking beyond the surface.

I included images from The Good Doctor to examine the "savant genius" trope, an Instagram post reinforcing the "lack of emotion" stereotype, iceberg and spectrum graphics that reveal how oversimplified educational visuals can still do harm, and direct quotes from autistic individuals discussing their own experiences. The final image, a doodle of someone speaking in an exaggerated, patronizing tone, connects media stereotypes to real-world behavior, showing how assumptions shape the way people actually treat autistic individuals.

## Rhetorical reflection

**Heading:**

Rhetorical Reflection

**Copy:**

The rhetorical power of this project comes from its format. Telar's zoom structure requires viewers to actively click through each section, which means they are not passive recipients of an argument. They are participants in it. Moving from surface-level recognition to deeper analysis mirrors the kind of critical thinking I wanted to model.

Choosing images from real media, including a TV show screenshot, a social media post, and an educational graphic, grounds the project in artifacts audiences have likely already seen. This makes the critique feel immediate and personal rather than abstract. The shift from outside portrayals to firsthand autistic voices in the middle of the project is the most important tonal move: it redirects authority away from media interpretation and toward autistic people themselves.

**Alt text pattern for slides:**

## Artist's statement

**Heading:**

Artist's Statement

**Copy:**

This project uses satire to do what a straightforward argument often cannot: make the audience feel the absurdity before they have had a chance to get defensive. I created an Instagram carousel framed as a mock "field guide to neurotypicals," using clinical language and documentary-style observation to describe common neurotypical behaviors the same way society describes autistic ones.

My audience was teens and young adults who regularly encounter simplified or stereotypical portrayals of autism through social media, so I met them where they are, using the same format that spreads misconceptions to challenge them. The carousel opens with humor and gradually shifts into something more serious, ending with real statistics about employment barriers and a direct reckoning with the consequences of these stereotypes. The satire is the hook; the sincerity is the point.

## Rhetorical reflection

**Heading:**

Rhetorical Reflection

**Copy:**

The mirror technique is the rhetorical engine of this project. By applying the same reductive, pseudoscientific logic to neurotypical people that society applies to autistic individuals, the early slides expose how strange and dehumanizing that logic actually sounds, but only if the audience is willing to sit with the discomfort.

Using overly clinical labels and observational framing was a deliberate choice to make the stereotyping legible as a method, not just a conclusion. The gradual tonal shift from satire to sincerity was equally intentional: humor earns attention on social media, but the slides on infantilization, unemployment, and underemployment are where the real argument lands. Visually, the muted colors, consistent typography, and documentary-style layout kept the satire feeling cohesive and intentional rather than mean-spirited. The design signals that this is commentary, not cruelty.
